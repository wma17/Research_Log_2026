Do They Know? Yes: "Err Knowingly"

PAPER A · Sec. 3.2, 4 · Fig. 6–10
The ability to tell is there. The final decision doesn't use it.
Evidence 1 · behaviour during reasoning
  • Insert stopping points in the thinking: at "wait" (fixation cases) or at a paragraph break (hallucinated cases).
  • At each stopping point, prompt the model to answer directly, or to explain why the question is unanswerable.
  • Result: in more than half of the fixation cases, some stopping point gives a correct "I don't know"; correct explanations at stopping points exceed 80% for both failure types.
Evidence 2 · hidden states
  • Linear probe p(unanswerable) = σ(⟨θ, x⟩) on the attention output of one layer.
  • Trained on 2,000 SUM pairs (+200 for validation), 1,000 token activations per question. A question's score = average over tokens so far.
  • Result: accuracy (answerable vs unanswerable) rises as reasoning goes on, above 0.8 by the end for most models.
Why no refusal? At the stopping points, the probability of the "I don't know" tokens is lower in the two failure types than in correct abstentions, and "I don't know" is produced less often. The signal exists but is not strong enough to stop the reasoning.
so: monitor the signal and act on it
[1:10] Do the models know? Two kinds of evidence. First, behaviour. They stop the reasoning at "wait" or at a paragraph break, and ask the model to answer right there, or to explain why the question can't be answered. In more than half of the fixation cases, some stopping point gives a correct "I don't know". And the explanations are correct more than 80 percent of the time. Second, hidden states. A linear probe on one layer's attention output predicts "unanswerable". Its accuracy grows during reasoning and passes 0.8 near the end. So why no refusal? The probability of the "I don't know" tokens is lower in the failure cases. The awareness is there, but too weak to stop the reasoning. That is what "err knowingly" means.

Paper B at a Glance: Trust-Score

PAPER B · Song et al., ICLR 2025 · "Measuring and Enhancing Trustworthiness of LLMs in RAG …"
In RAG, the model must answer only from the documents, with citations, or refuse.
Task
Input: question q + top-5 retrieved documents D. Output: statements with inline citations, "… [1][2] …", using only D. If D can't answer q: "I apologize, but I couldn't find an answer to your question in the search results."
Answerable or not?
q is answerable if some documents in D entail (by an NLI model) at least one gold claim. The label comes from the documents, not from the model.
Five error types
inaccurate answer · over-responsiveness (answers when it should refuse) · excessive refusal · over-citation · improper citation
TRUST = ⅓ ( F1GR + F1AC + F1GC )
scorewhat it checks
F1GRGrounded refusal: average of the refusal-F1 and the answer-F1. Does it refuse exactly the unanswerable questions?
F1ACAnswer correctness: share of gold claims recovered, counting only claims the documents contain.
F1GCCitation quality: do the cited documents support each statement (NLI), precision and recall.
AR%Answered ratio. Should match the share of answerable questions in the data.
Their finding: prompting (in-context examples) can't fix this; strong closed models (GPT-4) lean on parametric knowledge instead of the documents.
how Trust-Align trains the refusal
[1:10] Paper B, Trust-Align, ICLR 2025. The task: a question plus five retrieved documents. The model must answer only from the documents, with citations, or refuse with a fixed refusal sentence. A question counts as answerable if the documents entail at least one gold claim. Note that the label comes from the documents, not from what the model knows. They define five error types. The one we care about is over-responsiveness: answering when it should refuse. The metric, Trust-Score, averages three F1 scores: grounded refusal, answer correctness, and citation quality. AR is the share of questions answered. They find prompting doesn't fix it, and strong models lean on their own knowledge rather than the documents.

What Both Papers Leave Open

OUR READING
Both report how often the model refuses, not why it should refuse a given question.
Paper A · reasoning
  • One abstention rate over all unanswerable questions.
  • The probe learns "answerable vs not" from labels. Most of its gain is stopping runaway reasoning.
  • It doesn't separate "the question is missing a condition" from "the model can't solve it".
Paper B · RAG
  • "Answerable" is defined by the documents.
  • Refusal is a learned rule ("only use D"), not the model's uncertainty.
  • No comparison of the same question with and without retrieval.
Common gap
Existing work reports a total refusal rate or one confidence score (roughly a total uncertainty). Nobody splits the failure by its source.
Our scope
Start with reasoning on SUM: clean paired twins and a known missing condition. RAG comes later, once we have a "with vs. without retrieval" dataset.
Part 2: why should a model refuse?
[0:50] What the two papers leave open. Both measure how often the model refuses, not why it should refuse a particular question. In Paper A the probe is trained from labels, and most of its gain is early stopping. It doesn't tell apart a missing condition from a question the model simply can't solve. In Paper B, answerability is defined by the documents, and refusal is a learned rule. More generally, papers report a total refusal rate or one confidence number. Nobody splits the failure by source. We start with reasoning on SUM because it has clean twins with a known missing condition. RAG comes later.

Missing Condition vs. Cannot Solve

OUR ANALYSIS · EXAMPLES FROM SUM
Two different reasons not to trust an answer, and they need different actions.
Source 1 · the question is missing a condition → AU
AU (aleatoric uncertainty): uncertainty that sits in the question itself. No model can remove it.
SUM 008-u: "The graph G of y = log₁₀x is rotated counter-clockwise about the origin to obtain G′. What is the equation for G′?"
Original: "rotated 90°", answer y = 10−x. The answer depends on the angle.
Right action: refuse, or ask "by how many degrees?". Thinking longer cannot help.
Source 2 · the model cannot solve it → EU
EU (epistemic uncertainty): uncertainty from the model's missing knowledge or skill. A better model could remove it.
SUM 053-a: a complete competition problem about rolling tubes (answer 312).
R1-Distill-Qwen-7B: 0 of 20 samples correct, 20 nearly all different answers.
Right action: refuse, or use a tool, retrieval, or a stronger model.

so what do we measure, and what is the refusal?
[1:20] There are two reasons not to trust a math answer. Source one: the question is missing a condition. This is aleatoric uncertainty, AU: it sits in the question, and no model can remove it. Example: a graph is rotated counter-clockwise, but the angle is removed. The answer depends on the angle. The right action is to refuse or ask; thinking longer can't help. Source two: the question is complete but the model can't solve it. This is epistemic uncertainty, EU: it comes from the model. Example: a hard rolling-tubes problem; our 7B model gets 0 out of 20 samples right, with 20 different answers. Here a tool or a stronger model could help. On SUM we turn this into three labels: ill_posed for the unanswerable questions, cannot_solve for answerable questions the model mostly gets wrong, and fine.

Our Framing: Uncertainty → Refusal

OUR FRAMING
Uncertainty is the belief; refusal is the action taken from it.
What we measure
  • We do not compute the uncertainty of the text "I don't know".
  • We force the model to give an answer, sample many answers, and measure how spread out the answers are.
  • Then we ask whether the model's refusal follows that spread.
Refusal failure, in these terms
The forced answers are spread out (or depend on a missing value), but the model still commits to one answer.
RQ-A
Measurement
Can uncertainty tell a missing condition (AU) from cannot solve (EU), better than the model's own stated reason?
The stated ("verbal") reason is known to be unreliable, so it is only a baseline.
RQ-B
Decision
Is "refuse when uncertainty is high" a better rule than the model's own decision, the AAAI probe, or a length rule?
Measured as abstention on unanswerable questions vs. accuracy on answerable ones (a refusal counts as wrong).
Part 3: how to measure AU and EU
[1:10] Our framing separates belief and action. Uncertainty is the belief; refusing is an action taken because of it. So we don't compute the uncertainty of the words "I don't know". We force the model to answer, sample many answers, and measure the spread. Then we check whether the refusal follows the spread. A refusal failure is: the answers are spread out, or depend on a missing value, but the model commits anyway. We don't use the model's stated reason as the main signal, because verbal uncertainty is known to be unreliable. We keep it as a baseline. This gives two research questions. A, measurement: can uncertainty separate missing condition from cannot-solve? B, decision: is refusing on high uncertainty better than the model's own decision, the probe, or a length rule?

One Template for Uncertainty Decomposition

STANDARD BAYESIAN DECOMPOSITIONTABLE: OUR SUMMARY
Total = between-Z part + within-Z part. What the parts mean depends on what Z is.
H( Y | X )  =  I( Y ; Z | X )  +  EZ [ H( Y | X, Z ) ]
X = question, Y = answer, Z = an auxiliary variable. Left side: entropy of the mixture q(Y|X) = EZ[ q(Y|X, Z) ]. Red: how much the answer changes with Z. Blue: spread that remains once Z is fixed.

Rule of thumb: if Z changes the correct answer, variation across Z is about the question (AU). If Z keeps the correct answer, variation across Z is about the model (EU).
ICE uses Z = clarification
[1:20] Most Bayesian decompositions have the same form. Total entropy of the answer equals a mutual-information term, how much the answer changes with an auxiliary variable Z, plus an expected entropy, the spread left once Z is fixed. The meaning depends on what Z is. In a Bayesian neural network or deep ensemble, Z is the model parameters; models disagreeing is EU. In ICE, Z is a clarification of the input. A clarification changes the correct answer, so answers changing with Z means the question was under-specified: that's AU. The direction is reversed from the ensemble case. A paraphrase doesn't change the correct answer, so answers changing with a paraphrase is about the model. The rule of thumb is at the bottom.

ICE: Input Clarification Ensembling

Hou et al., "Decomposing Uncertainty for LLMs through Input Clarification Ensembling", ICML 2024
Ensemble over clarified inputs instead of over models.
1Clarify
An LLM writes K clarifications c₁ … cK of the input X. Each one resolves the ambiguity in one way.
→
2Predict
The target LLM answers X ⊕ ck (the input with ck appended), several samples for each k.
→
3Ensemble
Mixture q(Y|X) = (1/K) Σk q(Y | X ⊕ ck), then decompose its entropy.
H( q(Y|X) ) = I( Y ; C | X ) AU  +  EC H( q(Y | X ⊕ C) ) EU
Why the mutual information is AU here
Each clarification removes the input's ambiguity. If the answers still differ across clarifications, the original input was under-specified.
Why the remainder is EU on SUM
Once the missing value is supplied, a SUM question is well-posed (true AU = 0). The spread left within a clarification is the model's own uncertainty. ("Illusion of Certainty", 2025: at AU = 0, consistency-based entropy tracks EU.)
our own paper uses a different Z
[1:10] ICE, from ICML 2024. Three steps. An LLM writes several clarifications of the input; each resolves the ambiguity one way. The target model answers each clarified input several times. Then the answers are pooled into a mixture, and its entropy is decomposed. The mutual information between the answer and the clarification is AU: if the answers still differ across clarifications, the original input was under-specified. The expected entropy within a clarification is EU. This fits SUM well: once we supply the missing value, the problem is well-posed, so true AU is zero, and what is left inside each clarification is the model's own uncertainty. A 2025 paper, "The Illusion of Certainty", shows that at zero AU, consistency-based entropy tracks EU.

Our NeurIPS'26 Paper: Ensemble-of-Ensembles

OUR PAPER · "Black-Box UQ for LLMs via Ensemble-of-Ensembles", NeurIPS 2026 (with IBM)
A two-level ensemble for black-box models, decomposed by the law of total covariance.
1
Setting
Black-box API: only text in and text out. No weights, no hidden states, often no logits.
2
Outer ensemble
Meaning-preserving perturbations of the input. The input is the only handle we have on a black-box model.
3
Inner ensemble
Several sampled responses for each perturbed input.
4
Decompose in embedding space
Embed each response; split its total covariance into a within-perturbation and a between-perturbation part.
Cov[e] = Eo[ Cov(e | o) ] + Covo( E[e | o] )
e = response embedding, o = outer perturbation of the input.
How we checked the meaning
A 2×2 paired intervention on the same questions: adding a clarification lowers AU; adding evidence lowers EU.
What we take from it for this project
The template (law of total variance + an auxiliary variable) and the habit of validating the meaning of each part by intervention. Not the full two-level design.
why ICE fits this task better
[1:00] Our own NeurIPS paper solves the same decomposition problem in a black-box setting, where we only see text. The outer ensemble perturbs the input while keeping its meaning; the inner ensemble samples several responses per input. We embed the responses and split their total covariance into a within part and a between part, the law of total covariance. We checked the meaning with a 2 by 2 intervention: adding a clarification lowers AU, adding evidence lowers EU. For this project we keep the template and the habit of checking what each part means by intervening. We don't carry over the full two-level design. Next slide: why.

The task decides the auxiliary variable: for math, fill in the missing condition.

1Not black-boxWe run the 7B ourselves. Input perturbation is not our only handle, so we can choose the Z that fits the task.
2The natural Z for math is CC = an assignment of the missing value ("Note: the graph is rotated 45°."). A paraphrase of a math problem is brittle and can change the problem itself.
3ICE alone already splits AU / EUStacking a paraphrase layer on top adds cost and a second source of variance we cannot label.
4Discrete answers, not embeddingsMath answers are numbers or expressions. We group them by mathematical equivalence and use entropy over classes. Sentence embeddings barely separate "22.5" from "25", and break on intervals and units.
5SUM lets us check the meaningEvery unanswerable question has a twin with the true value, so we know the "right" C and when AU should be 0.
Price: sampling is expensive (5 C × 10 samples per question). We accept it for now: first get a measurement with a checkable meaning, then make it cheap.
the exact estimator
[1:20] Why ICE and not the full ensemble-of-ensembles. Five reasons. One: we are not black-box. We run the model ourselves, so the input is not our only handle. Two: for math, the natural auxiliary variable is filling in the missing value. Paraphrasing a math problem is fragile and can change the problem. Three: ICE already separates AU and EU. Adding a paraphrase layer costs more and adds variance we can't label. Four: math answers are discrete, so we group equivalent answers and compute entropy over classes. Embeddings don't separate numbers well. Five: SUM's twins let us check the meaning, because we know the true value. The price is cost: 50 samples per question. We accept that for now.

And the best rule uses the 27B's verdict, which is not the tested model's own uncertainty. That is the main open risk.

Part 5: where this leaves us
[1:40] G6 checks whether EU means "the model can't do it". The test uses only the unanswerable questions, split by whether the model can solve the answerable twin. That split comes from the twin's own samples, so it is independent. EU with oracle clarifications separates the two groups at 0.78; raw entropy only 0.62. The gain is 0.16 and robust. Even with generated clarifications it's +0.09. Example: two questions with the same raw entropy, 1.5; EU is 0.13 for one whose twin the model can solve, and 0.93 for one it can't. But it's not clean. AU should not depend on whether the model can solve the twin, but it does, because AU is capped by one minus EU: if the model scatters everywhere, AU can't show. And EU on the unanswerable question is higher than on the twin, because the alternative values make problems harder. There's also a possible leak: C0 re-asks the twin. We're checking both now.
