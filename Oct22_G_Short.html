[0:10] Part one. Why should we care about where the model decides?

A Walk Through a Tree

An LLM writes one word at a time, so every answer is one path through a tree.
▶ Sample a path or click a word in the middle column

So when we ask “how sure is the model?”, where do we look?
[1:00] Quick picture first. An LLM writes one word at a time. At each step there are a few likely next words, and it picks one. So any answer is just one path through a big tree. [Click "Sample a path" once or twice.] Most of these branches don't really matter. "Labyrinth" or "maze" — same story, different word. But some branches do matter. If the model takes the other branch, the final answer is different. The paper calls these words "forking tokens". A token is just a word or a piece of a word.

Where Do We Measure?

Usual confidence scores look at the finish line — after the choice is already made.
Q: Who is the current British head of state?
Let's think step by step. The current year is 2024
try the next word 2024 · 62% 2021 · 23% 2023 · 15%
Usual ways to measure confidence — all look at the end
probability of the answer wordverbalized: ask “how sure are you?”ask N times, check agreement
98%sure at the endvs3 : 1split before the year
The paper's bet: a few specific words are where the answer gets decided.
[1:30] Here's the paper's own example. The question is: who is the current British head of state? The model first writes: "The current year is…" and then picks a year. [Click 2024, then 2021.] If it writes 2024, it goes on to say King Charles. If it writes 2021, it says Queen Elizabeth. Now look at the confidence. At the end of either path, the model is about 98% sure. But before it wrote the year, it was more like a 3-to-1 split. And the usual ways we measure confidence — the probability of the answer word, verbalized confidence where we just ask "how sure are you?", or asking many times and checking agreement — they all look at the end. So they miss the moment where the choice was made.

[0:50] So the main idea is: change one word — even a word the model likes — and the whole answer can flip. They ask three questions. One: do these forks really exist, and how often? Two: which words turn out to be forks? Three: what does this mean for confidence scores? One rule they set for themselves: only use the API. No access to the model's insides, no training. [0:10] Part two. So how do they actually find these forks, using only the API?

The Answer Mix

At each word: “if we re-rolled from here, what would the model answer?”
keep the words so far→ finish it many times→ count the answers =answer mix ot
Real example (HotpotQA trivia) · Q: which actress born June 19, 1972 was in The In-Laws? · each bar = answer mix after that word
bars look alikethese words don't change the answer
flip at Miafrom here it's set on “Mia Sara” → a fork
How do we get this mix when all we have is an API?
[1:15] This is the key idea of the paper, so let me go slow. At each word, we ask: if we stopped here and let the model finish many times, what would it end up answering? We count those answers. I'll call that the "answer mix". In the paper it's called o-t. This is real data from a trivia question in HotpotQA: which actress born June 19, 1972 was in the movie The In-Laws? The right answer is Robin Tunney, in green. Each bar is the answer mix right after that word. For a long time the bars look alike — lots of mixed colors. Then the model writes "Mia", and suddenly almost everything is red: "Mia Sara", which is wrong. That sudden change is what a fork looks like.

The Recipe: 3 Steps

Brute force: re-roll from every word, many times, and count answers.

1Get one answer

Always take the most likely word
and note the other likely words at each step
… in question is Mia Sara. 2. Next …

2Branch & re-roll

At every word, try each likely option
(≥ 5% chance) and let the model finish 30 times

3Read the answer

A second LLM reads off the final answer
turn it into a one-hot vector, then count
“…is Robin Tunney.” → Robin Tunney
→ one-hot [1,0,0,0,0,0]
1 = this answer, 0 = all others → add up = counts
Next step ▸ model testedGPT-3.5
why it's pricey ~150 words×~2 options×30 re-rolls×~150 words each ≈ 1M tokens ≈ $2 per question
Let's zoom into one single word.
[1:15] How do they get this answer mix with only an API? Brute force. [Click "Next step" as you go.] Step one: get one answer by always taking the most likely word, and write down the other likely words at each step. Step two: at every word, try each likely option — anything with at least 5% chance — and let the model finish 30 times. Step three: the answers are free text, so a second, cheaper LLM reads off the final answer. Then we turn it into a one-hot vector: a 1 for that answer and 0 for all others. Add these up and you get the counts. The catch is cost. About 150 words, times about 2 options, times 30 re-rolls, times about 150 words each. That's around a million tokens, about two dollars, for one question. So they only use 30 questions per task, and only one model, GPT-3.5.

When does a change in the mix count as a real fork?
[1:15] Let's zoom in on one spot: where the model wrote "Mia". Each row is a word the model could have written here, with its chance. The bar shows what the 30 re-rolls answered. "Mia" has 43%, and it leads to Mia Sara or Mia Maestro — both wrong. But "Robin" has 14%, and it always gives the right answer. "None" — probably "none other than…" — also mostly gives the right answer. The bottom bar is the overall mix: all the rows added up, weighted by their chance. [Optional: click another word.] For most other words there's only one likely option, so nothing happens. The action is all in a few spots.

Test 1: Spot the Jump

Does the answer mix suddenly jump while the model writes? That jump is the fork.
how A standard stats tool (change-point detection) fits the blue line as flat stretches plus sudden jumps, and says how sure it is about each jump.
But what about forks the model didn't take?
[1:15] Now, how do we decide a change is a real fork and not just noise? Test one. Read the three panels in order. Panel one, the blue line: how far the answer mix has moved from where it started. Panel two, the red bars: where the jump most likely happened. Panel three: how many jumps there are, as the tool's best guess. The tool is a standard stats method called change-point detection. It treats the blue line as flat stretches plus sudden jumps. The rule is simple: if the chance of "no jump at all" is below 10%, we call it a fork. For this trivia question, that chance is zero — clear fork. [Click "Coin flip".] For the coin question, there's no real jump. The model knew the answer from the start.

Test 2: Roads Not Taken

At each word: could a likely other word have flipped the answer?
① Risk at each word
Chance the model picks a different word that would change the answer mix a lot.
risk = sum of the chances of those “dangerous” words
② Survival
Chance of reaching the end without hitting any dangerous word.
survival = (1 − risk₁) × (1 − risk₂) × …
what counts as “a lot” 0.60
“a lot” = 0.6a big change: even 50/50 → 85/15 doesn't count
small risks add up1% per word × 200 words → only 13% survive
Time for experiments: which tasks, and what do we count?
[1:15] Test one only looks at the path the model actually took. But what about forks it didn't take? So test two asks, at each word: could a likely other word have flipped the answer? The risk at a word is the chance of picking a different word that would change the answer mix a lot. Survival is the chance of getting to the end without ever hitting one of those risky words. You multiply "no fork here" across all the words. [Move the slider a bit.] The slider sets what counts as "a lot". At 0.6 it has to be a big change — even 50/50 to 85/15 isn't enough. And small risks add up. One percent per word over 200 words means only 13% of runs get through with no fork.

Surprising Forks

Sometimes the fork is a boring token: a bracket, a digit.
Is this rare, or everywhere?
[1:30] That fork makes sense — it's a name. But some forks are boring words. GSM8k math: near the end, if the model writes an open bracket, it ends with $3,528. If it writes "by", it ends with $504. Both are wrong. The right answer, $21, was the favorite at the start and then just disappears. [Click "Physics".] MMLU physics: the model writes a formula that actually gives 8.9. But then the first digit it writes is "9" — the familiar 9.8 — and that decides it. "9" had 54%, "8" had 29%. [Click "Coin flip".] And the easy coin question: flat. No fork at all. The authors' take: the model doesn't plan its answer ahead. It decides as it writes. If you say "hole" instead of "hotel" by mistake, you fix it. An LLM might just keep going with the hole.

So what does “the model is 95% sure” even mean?
[1:15] Is this rare? Not really. Red bars: share of questions with a real jump. On the reasoning tasks it's 27% to 63%. The easy coin task: zero. Gray bars: average survival at the end. Mostly low, around 13% to 33%. Where do forks happen? Early for LastLetter. In the middle, during reasoning, for HotpotQA, GSM8k and AQuA. And right when the answer is written for MMLU, LastLetter and AQuA. Two quick checks: forks are not just unlikely words — often they're very likely words. And one odd thing: test one and test two don't agree at all. The coin task has no jumps, but survival is only 20%. Remember that — I'll come back to it on slide 21.

Confidence Can Mislead

Ask at the start: ~25% right. Ask at the end: ~100% sure of a wrong answer.
Confidence
It changes as the model writes — the number depends on when you ask.
Evaluation
A test score can hinge on one random word.
Safety
One word can tip the model into a wrong or harmful answer.
Okay — how much should we trust all this?
[1:00] So what does "the model is 95% sure" even mean? Same two questions. If you ask 300 times from scratch, the right answer is on top: about 25% for trivia, about 63% for math. If you ask at the end, after the reasoning — which is what the usual scores do — the model is about 100% sure of a wrong answer. Neither number is a bug. They measure different moments. Three takeaways: confidence changes as the model writes, so the number depends on when you ask. A test score can hinge on one random word. And one word can tip the model into a wrong or harmful answer.

[0:10] Last part. How much should we trust this, and what's next?

Limitations

Cool idea, small evidence: one model, 30 questions per task, settings picked by eye.
The authors admit
  • Expensive — ~1M tokens per question
  • One model — GPT-3.5 only
  • Jump detector sees jumps in noise → tuned by hand
  • Settings chosen because they “looked right”
  • Answer reader slips — “Mía” ≠ “Mia” Maestro
We'd ask
  • AU or EU? Is the question unclear (AU), or does the model just not know (EU)?
  • Cherry-picked? The “surprising” forks are hand-picked
  • Only one path checked per question
  • So what? Does it give better confidence scores?
  • Newer models fix themselves (“wait…”) — do forks remain?
One more thing we found by reading their code…
[1:15] How much should we trust this? It's a cool idea, but the evidence is small. What the authors admit: it's expensive, it's only GPT-3.5, the jump detector sometimes sees jumps in noise so they tuned it by hand, and some settings were picked because they "looked right". Also the answer reader slips: in their data, "Mía Maestro" with an accent and "Mia Maestro" count as different answers. What we'd ask: First, AU or EU? Is a fork there because the question is unclear — aleatoric uncertainty — or because the model just doesn't know — epistemic? The Mia Sara case is clearly the second. Second, the "surprising" forks are hand-picked. Third, they only check one path per question. Fourth, so what — does any of this give better confidence scores? They don't test that. And last, newer models fix themselves with "wait…" — do forks still matter?

Reading the Code

Their “low survival” number comes from a quirk in the code.
the quirk
Before comparing two words' answer mixes, the code multiplies each by how likely the word is.
coin example
Two words, same answer “No”.
One is 85% likely, the other 15%.
→ code sees a big gap → counts a fork.
explainscoin task: no jumps, yet survival only 20%
Where did this idea go next — and what can we do with it?
[1:30] One more thing. I went through their public code and data. Remember the odd result: the coin task has no jumps but low survival. Here's why. In the survival test, before comparing two words' answer mixes, the code multiplies each one by how likely that word is. Take the coin task. Two words both lead to the same answer, "No". But one word is 85% likely and the other is 15%. After multiplying, they look very different, so the code counts a fork — even though the answer never changes. Gray bars: their code. I get the same numbers as their table. Green bars: the way the paper's own formula says to do it. Survival goes way up, to 46% to 93%. So the "low survival" headline mostly comes from this quirk. To be fair, they might have a different version internally. But the lesson is: read the code.

[1:15] Where did this idea go? Three follow-ups. Critical Tokens Matter, ICML 2025: find the words that cause wrong answers and train the model to avoid them. Beyond the 80/20 Rule, NeurIPS 2025: they even call them "forking tokens". Doing RL only on the 20% most uncertain words gives better math scores. Thought Anchors: the same re-roll idea, but per sentence. Planning sentences steer the rest. Ideas for us: one, skip the re-rolls and predict forks from the model's insides in one pass. Two, re-roll smarter — spend the budget only where forks are likely. Three, AU vs EU at a fork. Four, act at the fork — look things up or double-check right before a risky word.
